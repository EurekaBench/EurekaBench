You are a systems neuroscientist studying how the nervous system of C. elegans decides between its two primary modes of locomotion. Its crawling is a sequence of forward and reversal states punctuated by turns, and the constant switching between them is what lets the animal move toward attractive stimuli, escape noxious ones, and explore or exploit its surroundings. Experimental work has identified a set of premotor interneurons as the drivers of these movements: one group, led by the command neurons AVB, drives forward crawling, another, led by the command neurons AVA, drives reversals, and all of them are recurrently wired to one another and receive input from over a hundred sensory neurons and interneurons.

Recordings of these interneurons in behaving animals show the forward-associated cells rising and falling together, the reversal-associated cells doing the same, and the two groups competing for dominance over the behavioral output, so that the difference between the two group averages tracks the animal's velocity. The joint activity holds the forward-high or the reversal-high configuration steadily and switches between them at irregular intervals of seconds to tens of seconds, with no manipulated stimulus timing a switch and with a pattern that varies from recording to recording, from frequent brief runs that keep the animal in place to long runs that carry it across its arena. Beneath this variety the recordings carry a fixed structure: the joint activity is low-dimensional, the two configurations behave as stable states that the activity rests in, each switch is driven by an unidentified biological signaling source, and the irregular dwell times and seemingly random order of the switches need no noise to arise, emerging instead from fully deterministic dynamics.

However, these descriptions live above the circuit, and it is unclear how the premotor interneurons work together to produce the behavior: what holds a group of distinct cells in a shared state, what makes the two groups exclude each other, what a switch is at the level of cells and connections, and where its irregular timing comes from.

Your task is to discover the mechanism that explains this phenomenon. Concretely, the mechanism has to account for how the premotor interneurons, wired to one another and to the rest of the nervous system as the connectome dictates, hold the forward and the reversal state and produce the irregular switching between them, and it has to do so from the quantities the simulator reports, the connections a cell makes and what arrives over them, rather than from the identity of a run or a memorized schedule of switches, so that it predicts the premotor activity of a run it never saw.
