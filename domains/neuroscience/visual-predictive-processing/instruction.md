You are a visual neuroscientist studying how the visual system of a person looking at a natural scene anticipates the content of one location from the scene around it. Only a small part of the retinal image is resolved at any moment, so the population of neurons that responds to a given location is driven both by the light inside its receptive field and by what the surrounding context implies should be there.

Recordings of the human visual cortex show that the response to one part of a scene is weaker when the content of that part is easy to anticipate from the scene around it and stronger when it is not. The effect is there during ordinary viewing, with no expectation imposed on the observer and nothing repeated for them to learn. 

However, it is unclear at what level of abstraction the visual system anticipates the content of a location, and what sets that level for a given population of neurons.

Your task is to discover the mechanism that explains this phenomenon. Concretely, the mechanism has to account for how much the context of a patch constrains its content at each level of abstraction, from the edges and fine structure inside it to the object it is part of, and which of those levels bears on the response of one population of neurons, and it has to do so from the quantities the simulator reports for that population rather than from where in the cortex that population sits, so that it predicts the response of a population it was never fitted on. To discover this, you interrogate the TRIBE simulator of the human cortical response, which answers a video, an audio recording or a text you hand it with the response it predicts over the whole cortical surface, by building those inputs yourself and querying it as often as your investigation needs, with the part of a scene, the scene around it, how well the two agree and where in the frame they sit all under your control.

Every claim in your discovered mechanism needs to be backed by data you actually collected. Your mechanism is evaluated through its executable form on held-out settings you never ran and a panel of cortical locations whose identities are never given to it, scored on how much of the response to a part of a scene sitting inside its surround it accounts for beyond what the responses to the two of them on their own already carry, on how far it holds each location back as the surround comes to imply that part, and on whether it places each location at the level of abstraction the recording places it at. Your experimental findings will also be reviewed by an independent researcher who has access to the same simulator, to assess whether the mechanism you discover can yield new insights.

Deliverable 2.
Write the executable form of your mechanism to /workspace/mechanisms/mechanism.py. It defines these four names, and whatever helpers they need. `PROPERTIES` names the entries of `props` your mechanism reads. `COEFFS` is a sequence of your fitted constant values, at most 10 numbers. `predict_response(props, coeffs)` returns an (N, P, T) numpy array of the predicted response of the P panel locations in each of the N held-out settings. `fit_coeffs(props, response, coeffs_init)` returns the refitted constant values, from the (N, P, T) recorded response of those locations, and it changes the constants only and leaves the structure of the computation unchanged.

`props` is a dict of numpy arrays. `"t"` (T,) is the shared time grid in seconds. `"surround"` (N, P, T) is the response recorded at those same locations when only the scene around the part was shown, `"part"` (N, P, T) is the response when only that part was shown on its own, `"implied"` (N, P, T) is the response when only the part that the scene around it implies was shown on its own, and `"baseline"` (N, P, T) is the response when none of them was shown. The response you predict, the response `fit_coeffs` is given and the four response entries of `props` are the same quantity in the same units, the cortical response the simulator reports.

The file is not told which population a row is and predicts each population from its own entries alone. Besides `props` and the constants you fit it may hold only physical constants and values fixed across all scenes and populations, so no lookup table, no learned component, no memorized per-scene value, and no cortical area label, population or voxel identity, row index, scene identity, subject identity or random seed. It is deterministic and imports only numpy and scipy. `predict_response` returns within two minutes and `fit_coeffs` within ten.
