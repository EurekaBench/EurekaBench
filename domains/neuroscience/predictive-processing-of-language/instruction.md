You are a cognitive neuroscientist studying how the human brain predicts upcoming language while a person listens to a story. Speech arrives at two to five words per second, and comprehension keeps up with it by anticipating both the next word and the next sentence.

Recordings of people listening to stories show that the brain's response to each word scales with how unexpected that word is in its context, and that higher-order cortical regions predict further ahead and more abstractly than the temporal cortices. Predictions of upcoming words engage the superior and middle temporal cortices, while predictions of upcoming sentences engage the right temporoparietal junction, the medial prefrontal cortex and the precuneus. 

However, it is unclear how the brain integrates the continuously arriving word-level information and uses it to update its sentence-level prediction, thereby adaptively responding to expected or surprising language inputs.

Your task is to discover the mechanism that explains this phenomenon. Concretely, the mechanism has to explain how word-level information accumulates and updates the response of a cortical location during a narrative, from what the simulator reports for that location rather than where it sits, so that it predicts narratives it was never fitted on. To discover this, you interrogate the TRIBE simulator of the human cortical response with narratives you write yourself.

Every claim in your discovered mechanism needs to be backed by data you actually collected. Your mechanism is evaluated through its executable form on 100 held-out narratives you never ran, both as submitted and refit by your own fitting function, on how well it reproduces the recorded response and its temporal structure. Your experimental findings will also be reviewed by an independent researcher who has access to the same simulator, to assess whether the mechanism you discover can yield new insights.

Deliverable 2.
Write the executable form of your mechanism to /workspace/mechanisms/mechanism.py. It defines these four names, and whatever helpers they need. `PROPERTIES` names the entries of `props` your mechanism reads. `COEFFS` is a sequence of your fitted constant values, at most 10 numbers. `predict_response(props, coeffs)` returns an (N, P, T) numpy array of the predicted response of the P panel locations in each of the N held-out narratives. `fit_coeffs(props, response, coeffs_init)` returns the refitted constant values, from the (N, P, T) recorded response of those locations, and it changes the constants only and leaves the structure of the computation unchanged.

`props` is a dict of numpy arrays. `"t"` (T,) is the shared time grid in seconds. `"rate"` (N, T) is the number of words each narrative delivers in each second. `"novelty"` (N, T) is how unexpected the words in each second are given the words before them. `"scrambled"` (N, P, T) is the response recorded at the same P locations when the words of that narrative are presented in a scrambled order at the same rate. The response you predict, the response `fit_coeffs` is given and `"scrambled"` are the same quantity in the same units, the cortical response the simulator reports.

The file is not told which cortical location a row is, which narrative a setting is, or what its words were; it may use the panel as a whole but never the identity of any location in it. Besides `props` and the constants you fit it may hold only mathematical constants and values fixed across all narratives and locations: no lookup table, no learned component, no memorized per-narrative value, and no location identity, row index, narrative identity, sampling period or random seed. It is deterministic and imports only numpy and scipy. `predict_response` returns within two minutes and `fit_coeffs` within ten.
