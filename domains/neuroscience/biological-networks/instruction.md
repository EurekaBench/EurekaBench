You are a systems neuroscientist studying how C. elegans converts the odor signal it samples into its next behavioral action, and how experience alters that conversion. The animal climbs an odor gradient with two strategies at once. It bends its heading toward the side where the odor is stronger, and it launches sharp reorientations at a rate set by how the odor concentration changes along its path. Butanone, a volatile compound of its bacterial food, is transduced mainly by a single sensory neuron class, which hands it to a first layer of interneurons that the salt and temperature senses also feed.

Behavioral and imaging studies of this pathway found that pairing butanone with food leaves C. elegans climbing the same gradient more strongly, and pairing the odor with starvation leaves it climbing less, hours after the training is over. The persistent change in sensory-motor response is stored in the neural circuits. Studies show that a remembered salt concentration or temperature already inverts the response of the first-layer interneuron that the sensory cells project to, while after an odor is encountered during starvation, the sensory neurons answer as before and the cells close to the motor output are the ones the new preference alters. They also show that in a circuit small enough to write down the same shift in climbing comes out with that change placed at either site. Groups given different training were driven with identical odor stimulation and answered with different action commands. 

However, it is unclear how the circuit converts the odor drive into the two action signals in the first place, and what changes within that conversion when learning occurs, so that the same drive entering the same sensory cells produces a different reorientation command and a different steering signal in one trained group than in another.

Your task is to discover the mechanism that explains this phenomenon. Concretely, the mechanism has to account for what in the odor drive each of the two action signals follows, and for what a learned change moves in that account. It has to do so from what the simulator delivers, the stimulus and an index that names the learning state without saying what was done to the circuit, so that it predicts a stimulation protocol it was never fitted on. To discover this, you interrogate the c302 model of the C. elegans nervous system through controlled numerical experiments designed to identify what reaches the cells that carry the two action signals, how each signal follows the recent history of the drive, and which part of that conversion each learning state moves. The structure of your predictive form needs to follow from the mechanism you uncover, and for each ingredient you explain why it is included, what it traces in the circuit, and how it shapes the action. The two action signals are the two navigation decisions of C. elegans. The reorientation command is the drive to launch a sharp turn that reverses or redirects the body, and the steering signal is the drive to bend the heading toward one side during a run. Both are quantities of the timescale the conversion works on rather than of a membrane potential, so the simulator reads each of them from the cells that carry it through a causal filter of 200 ms, and the evaluation reads them the same way.

Every claim in your discovered mechanism needs to be backed by data you actually collected. Its constants are refit by your own fitting function before it is scored.
