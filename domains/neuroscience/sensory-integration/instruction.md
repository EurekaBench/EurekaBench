You are a cognitive neuroscientist studying how the human cerebral cortex combines the information that arrives through different senses. A single event in the world usually reaches the brain through more than one sense at the same time. The sight of a hammer striking a nail and the sound of the impact carry information about the same event, so the cortex can put them together into one estimate of what happened and where it happened.

Behavioural experiments find that a person judges such an event more accurately when information from both senses is available than when either sense provides it alone. Through functional brain imaging (fMRI), we can measure the BOLD response across cortical regions, where each voxel represents a population of cortical neurons. A voxel or brain region often responds to a combined audiovisual stimulus more strongly than the sum of their responses to its two parts; this excess is largest when each part on its own drives the same voxel or brain region  weakly. Across the human cortex, however, the same comparison has yielded inconsistent results, with the average amplitude of a region's response to a combined stimulus often no greater than the sum of its unisensory responses. 

However, it is unclear how the enhancement produced by combining the two senses is expressed in the cortical response to a combined stimulus.

Your task is to discover the mechanism that explains this phenomenon. Concretely, the mechanism has to account for how the response of a cortical location to a combined audiovisual stimulus follows from the responses of that same location to each sense alone, and it has to do so from those responses themselves, which are quantities reported by the simulator, rather than from where that location sits in the cortex, so that it can predict the response at a location it was never fitted on. To discover this, you interrogate the TRIBE simulator of the human cortical response through controlled numerical experiments in which you build every stimulus yourself and compare what each sense alone evokes with what the two evoke together.

Every claim in your discovered mechanism needs to be backed by data you actually collected. Your mechanism is evaluated through its executable form on 100 held-out audiovisual settings you never ran, using a panel of cortical locations whose identities are never provided to it. It is evaluated once with the constants you submit and once with those constants refit by your own fitting function on a disjoint half of the data, while the structure of the mechanism remains fixed. Each criterion is a number between 0 and 1 that reads only your prediction against the recording. The criteria evaluate, separately, how much of the two senses together a cortical location lets through, which of the two senses it favours, how much of its level with neither sense present survives when both senses arrive, and where the combined response departs from anything that location's own responses account for. Your experimental findings will also be reviewed by an independent researcher who has access to the same simulator, to assess whether the mechanism you discover can yield new insights.
