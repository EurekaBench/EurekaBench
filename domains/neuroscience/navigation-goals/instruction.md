You are a systems neuroscientist studying how C. elegans makes its way to the source of an odor. Its path is built from two elements driven by one scalar quantity, the concentration its nose samples along the path: a gentle bending of the heading while it runs forward, which is steering and is driven by the steering signal, and an occasional large reorientation that throws the worm off in a new direction, which is a turn and is driven by the reorientation command. C. elegans is not thought to hold a representation of where the source lies or of which way it is pointing, and yet it comes out of a bout of turns already headed towards the source. Both signals are quantities of the timescale this conversion works on rather than of a membrane potential, so the simulator reads each of them from the cells that carry it through a causal filter of 200 ms, and the evaluation reads them the same way.

The concentration sampled at the nose splits into a part that swings with the head and sets how the worm bends its heading, and a part that does not and sets how often it reorients; closed-loop stimulation of a single first-layer interneuron pair with these two parts is enough to make a worm find a gradient and track it. A circuit no larger than an ON and an OFF sensory cell acting with opposite sign on the neck motor neurons reproduces the steering within a single head swing but not the bouts of turns, while a controller that learns on its own to stimulate a set of neurons drives the worm to a goal with a rule that differs with where in the circuit it is coupled.

However, it is unclear whether what C. elegans does next is settled by the odor it has just sampled or passes through something in the worm that outlasts either action, and it is unclear how a bout of turns ends with C. elegans pointing the right way when nothing the worm is thought to carry through such a bout says where the source is.

Your task is to discover the mechanism that explains this phenomenon. Concretely, the mechanism has to account for what decides, at each moment and over what history of the drive, which of the two actions the same odor drive produces and how strongly, for what carries that decision from one sample to the next, and for what ends a bout of reorientations, doing so from what the simulator delivers, the stimulus alone, so that it predicts a stimulation protocol it was never fitted on. To discover this, you interrogate the c302 model of the C. elegans nervous system through controlled numerical experiments.
